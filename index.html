00 / Statement Artist Statement

An old motif, repeated without end, becomes a surface with its own breath.

I am an Iranian-born artist working at the intersection of traditional ornament and generative systems. My work begins with a simple observation: the repeated motif — wave, star, scale, mesh — has always been a form of computation, long before computers.

Japanese Patterns renders eight traditional Japanese motifs — Seigaiha (waves), Asanoha (hemp leaf), Shippou (seven treasures), Kikkou (tortoise shell), Sayagata (interlocking keys), Uroko (fish scales), Tatewaku (rising steam), and Ichimatsu (checkerboard) — on a 60 × 60 grid. Each motif has its own shape vocabulary and its own distribution rule.

A soft palette is drawn from one of six curated colour sets — blues, greens, earth tones, purples, ocean blues, or sunset tones — and rendered at three levels of transparency. A subtle gradient and occasional outline give the composition depth. Each piece is defined by a numeric seed and can be regenerated, documented, and adapted for print, textile, and surface applications.

On AI tools — where I encountered technical obstacles, I turned to AI tools for debugging and code optimization. Every structural, aesthetic, and conceptual decision remained my own.

01 / Motifs

Eight Traditions

Eight motifs are available, each drawn from a different historical tradition: Seigaiha (waves), Asanoha (hemp leaf), Shippou (seven treasures), Kikkou (tortoise shell), Sayagata (interlocking keys), Uroko (fish scales), Tatewaku (rising steam), and Ichimatsu (checkerboard).

02 / Palettes

Six Soft Sets

Each seed picks one of six curated palettes: soft blues, gentle greens, warm earth tones, soft purples, ocean blues, or sunset tones. Every palette pairs a light background hue with three or four companion colours, all rendered at one of three transparency levels for depth.

03 / Depth

Gradient & Outline

A very subtle gradient overlay sits behind the pattern, darkening toward the bottom edge. For three of the motifs (Asanoha, Kikkou, Uroko), a faint outline traces the shape at a slightly larger scale — giving the pattern crispness without introducing new colours.
